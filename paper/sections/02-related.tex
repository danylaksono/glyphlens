\section{Related work}
\label{sec:related}

The closest precedents to a bearing-faithful summarising lens are mostly not
called lenses. They sit in five literatures: interactive lenses and their design
spaces; labelling around a focus region; off-screen indicators on the viewport
border; radial and necklace cartography; and glyph maps with their underlying
local statistics.

\subsection{Interactive lenses and their design spaces}

Magic lenses were introduced as movable see-through filters that modify the
presentation of the objects beneath them~\cite{bier1993toolglass}; a taxonomy of
see-through tools followed~\cite{bier1994taxonomy}, and composing overlapping
lenses was addressed as a programming-model problem~\cite{fox1998composing}.
Tominski et al.'s surveys model a visualization lens as a pipeline attached to
the visualization pipeline through a selection $\Sel$ and a join $\Jn$, with a
lens function $\lambda$ between them (the symbol is introduced in the extended
version), classify lenses by data type and task, and add an \emph{effect
extent}---lens interior, side effects, separate view---to earlier
axes~\cite{tominski2014star,tominski2017survey}. Among their future directions
are flexibly combining lenses and a unified lens toolkit~\cite{tominski2014star,
tominski2017survey}; both are directions this work speaks to. Mota et al.\ derive
a seven-dimension design space for spatially embedded lenses from 45 papers
(2006--2020): position, orientation and scale; shape; dimensionality; effect
scope (interior, exterior, separate view); effect imagery; effect encoding
(thematic or spatially based); and viewpoint dependency~\cite{mota2025design}.
Two of these dimensions have no value for a necklace ring: its effect sits
neither inside, outside nor apart from the lens but \emph{on its boundary},
and its encoding is thematic marks under a spatial layout.

Lenses on maps have mostly magnified, filtered or re-rendered: JellyLens adapts
its shape to the features under it~\cite{pindat2012jellylens}; MoleView pushes
elements outside an attribute range towards the lens
border~\cite{hurter2011moleview}; ClusterLens re-aggregates points inside the lens
at a finer resolution~\cite{zhang2020clusterlens}; route detail lenses place
magnified views around the map border in route order, with leader lines only to
the first and last~\cite{karnick2010route}; and the Sampling Lens samples points
inside a region to reduce clutter, listing aggregate statistics as future
work~\cite{ellis2005sampling}. VectorLens selects curves by position, diameter,
direction and angular tolerance, and draws the selected angular ranges on a ring
around the lens---a ring whose angle means direction, used for selection rather
than display~\cite{dumas2015vectorlens}. The name GlyphLens already belongs to a
view-dependent lens that pulls occluding 3D glyphs aside~\cite{tong2017glyphlens}.

Closer to our subject are lenses and probes that \emph{summarise}. Probes pair a
user-defined region with a pane of local charts, linked to it by a line or a
shared colour and placed by the user~\cite{butkiewicz2008probes}; a follow-up
alerts analysts when regions being compared differ unfairly on some dimension, and
offers semi-automatic boundary edits, while explicitly setting the scale
component of the modifiable areal unit problem aside~\cite{butkiewicz2010maup}.
TrajectoryLenses combine map lenses with set operations and show aggregated
attributes in the vicinity of the lens or in colour-matched
panels~\cite{kruger2013trajectorylenses}; a traffic-flow selection widget filters
by area and direction and shows the selection in annotation
windows~\cite{scheepens2016traffic}; GTMapLens browses geo-tagged text with movable
lenses~\cite{ma2020gtmaplens}. The closest academic precedent we found is the
\emph{time lens} of Tominski et al.: a circular query area on a trajectory map,
with a ring segmented into cyclic time bins whose fill shows the trajectories
inside the circle~\cite{tominski2012stacking}. There, angle means time.
Outside the literature, VisQuill ships map lenses that aggregate the data beneath them into a
bar chart around the rim~\cite{visquill}; its documentation does not say what
angle encodes, and its open-source lens blueprint spaces one bar per category
evenly along the arc.

\subsection{Marks around a focus region}

\emph{Excentric labelling} shows the labels of objects inside a cursor-centred
circle, connected to them by lines and updated as the cursor
moves~\cite{fekete1999excentric}. Labels are laid out in left and right stacks;
in its radial variant bearing is used only to order them. When there are too
many objects it shows a count and a sample of labels, and a glyph ``summarizing
the contents of the area'' is proposed as future work~\cite{fekete1999excentric}.
\emph{Extended excentric labelling} adds support for high and uneven density and
for summary statistics~\cite{bertini2009extended}
\doubt{what those statistics are and how they are drawn is unverified: full text
not accessed}.

The algorithmic literature on \emph{focus-region} and \emph{contour} labelling
places labels on the boundary of a circular focus
itself~\cite{fink2012focus,bekos2019external}. Fink et al.\ connect each label
to its site by a straight or Bézier leader; in their radial model a label's port
is the radial projection of its site onto the circle, and sites too close in
angle are resolved by keeping a maximum conflict-free subset; a facility-location
variant clusters the sites and labels one representative per cluster; and the
focus region's position can itself be optimised~\cite{fink2012focus}. Haunert and
Hermes require every leader to point at the centre, allow one position per
label, and solve the resulting maximum-weight independent set in real time in a
browser~\cite{haunert2014labeling}. Boundary labelling has been extended to
moving focus regions~\cite{heinsohn2014boundary}, to dense point sets explored
at a fixed map scale with labels placed along the map's lower
edge~\cite{gedicke2021zoomless}, to fisheye displacement of labels
out of a focus with optimisation once it stops~\cite{niedermann2019focus}, and to
labels drawn as arcs in an annular orbit around a circle with minimum total
leader length~\cite{bonerath2024orbital}, where a study with 54 participants found
straight leaders faster than orbital-radial ones at similar
accuracy~\cite{wallinger2026clarity}.
\doubt{The version of record's abstract (checked 2026-09-25) confirms similar
accuracy and faster straight leaders. The participant count, $n=54$, was read
in arXiv v1.}

This is the geometric twin of what we do, and we build on it rather than around
it. The difference is in what the marks are and how conflicts are resolved. In
every one of these techniques a label stands for one object, a leader is always
drawn, and a conflict with the bearing is resolved either by \emph{selection}
(dropping a label) or by freeing the label's position. A summarising lens
places \emph{aggregates}, keeps every one of them, displaces them
\emph{minimally} from their bearing, and draws a leader only where the
resulting gap is large enough to mislead---so the leader's length is the
residual. We did not find a labelling technique that draws leaders conditionally
on displacement.

\paragraph{Off-screen indicators.} The same geometry appears at the edge of the
viewport. Off-screen techniques such as Halo, City Lights, EdgeRadar, Wedge and
Ambient Grids draw proxies at or near the viewport border for objects beyond it,
and all of them encode those objects' direction; EdgeRadar and Ambient Grids
compress the off-screen space into a dedicated border region, and Ambient Grids
aggregates it into grid cells~\cite{baudisch2003halo,zellweger2003citylights,
gustafson2007edgeradar,gustafson2008wedge,jackle2015ambient,jackle2017topology}.
How a proxy's position should relate to its object is not settled. In a study
with 18 participants on a rectangular border, 94.4\% of localisation judgements
assumed an orthographic rather than a radial projection, with lower error for
orthographic~\cite{jackle2017topology}; the authors note that radial projection is
the familiar one when there is a point of interest, as in car navigation. A lens
boundary is circular and its centre is exactly such a point, so the result
transfers only in part; we return to it in \cref{sec:evaluation}.
\doubt{The off-screen techniques other than Jäckle et al.\ 2017 are cited from
their registry records and from Jäckle et al.'s classification, not from their
own texts.}

CompaRing copies a handful of objects into the slots of a ring for comparison and
gives each slot an arc that points towards the object's original location, wider
for objects further away~\cite{tominski2016comparing}; laying the slots out by
the objects' directions was suggested by the experts it was shown to, but not
built. Ring-shaped maps have also been proposed for egocentric navigation in
augmented reality~\cite{danyluk2026ringmaps}.

\subsection{Radial and necklace cartography}

Radial layouts are surveyed by Draper et al.~\cite{draper2009radial}. The
direct ancestor of bearing-faithful placement is the \emph{necklace map}: the
regions of a map are projected onto intervals of a curve that surrounds them,
and proportional symbols are placed without overlap inside their
intervals~\cite{speckmann2010necklace}. The curve may be any star-shaped curve;
a region's interval can be a fixed-width window around the bearing of its
centroid or the wedge it subtends from the centre; and several nested or
disjoint necklaces may share a map. The objective is to maximise a common symbol
scale, after which symbols are pushed towards the middle of their intervals by a
force-based step. For a fixed order this is solvable in $O(n\log n)$; with
arbitrary order it is NP-hard for wedge intervals, and ordering symbols by the
bearing of their regions is \emph{not} always optimal for that
objective~\cite{speckmann2015algorithms}. Necklace maps explicitly do without
leaders, relying on proximity and colour, and acknowledge that the association
between a symbol and its region is weaker than on other maps---``Interactivity
can help to strengthen this association''~\cite{speckmann2010necklace}.

\emph{Ring maps} arrange evenly spaced ``spokes'', one per areal unit, in rings
of attributes around a base map, and tie every spoke to its unit with a leader
line; their authors name the limited representation of spatial topology in the
rings as the main limitation and point to interactive ring maps as the
remedy~\cite{stewart2011ringmaps}.

A necklace lens differs from both in three ways. It is interactive and local---a
movable selection rather than a whole map. Its placement minimises squared
displacement from each mark's preferred bearing at a mark size fixed by the ring,
rather than maximising a common scale within intervals. And its leaders are
conditional: drawn where the gap between mark and data is large, absent where
adjacency suffices---a middle course between the necklace map's no leaders and
the ring map's leader for every spoke.

\subsection{Glyph maps, within-cell structure and local statistics}

Glyph placement strategies divide into data-driven and
structure-driven~\cite{ward2002taxonomy}; necklace placement is structure-driven
layout with a data-driven preferred position. Glyph design guidance is well
surveyed~\cite{borgo2013glyph}, but controlled evidence on glyph layout and on
glyphs over maps is thin~\cite{fuchs2017glyph}. Glyph-maps place small temporal
glyphs at the data's own grid locations~\cite{wickham2012glyphmaps};
\emph{tilemaps} and \emph{gridded glyphmaps} aggregate into a regular grid and
draw a multivariate glyph per cell~\cite{slingsby2018tilemaps,slingsby2023gridded,
laksono2024gridded}, and multivariate glyph placement with level of detail uses
hierarchical centroids~\cite{mcnabb2019multivariate}. Tilemaps were proposed as a
way to summarise the outputs of geographically weighted statistics, with the
grid interactively resized and panned to expose the modifiable areal unit
problem and a distance-decay kernel larger than the tiles as an
option~\cite{slingsby2018tilemaps}. Gridded glyphmaps keep the grid fixed in screen
space and re-aggregate on zoom, and name glyphs that convey within-cell
heterogeneity as future work~\cite{slingsby2023gridded}.

That heterogeneity is what two hexagon techniques encode. Honeycomb Plots
observe that colour-coded hexbins cannot distinguish ``uniform distributions
within tiles \ldots from clusters or trends if their numbers of points match'',
and cut each tile along the regression plane of its within-tile density; a
between-subjects study with 42 participants found strong evidence for slope
tasks~\cite{trautner2022honeycomb}. HexTiles derive a per-tile confidence from
weighted within-tile variance, argued as mitigation of the modifiable areal unit
problem~\cite{kawakami2024hextiles}; its weighting is described only in words,
and its user study (seven participants) tested the tiles without the variance
encoding. Polar histograms of street bearings summarise a city's orientation
order~\cite{boeing2019urban}; they bin the bearing \emph{of} edges rather than
bearing \emph{from} a chosen centre, which is the quantity a lens bins.

\paragraph{Local statistics and scale.} A disc-shaped lens evaluated at many
centres is a moving-window statistic. Geographically weighted summary
statistics compute local summaries with a spatial kernel around each
location~\cite{brunsdon2002gwss}, in the family that began with geographically
weighted regression~\cite{brunsdon1996gwr}. Geographically weighted
visualization makes them interactive, calls the GW mean ``simply a moving
spatial window mean smoother'', plots summary statistics across bandwidths as a
\emph{scalogram}, notes that the statistics ``could \ldots be centred on any
points, such as those comprising a regular grid'', and adds \emph{directed}
weighting, in which a kernel is sharpened towards a principal direction at
thirty-degree clock points~\cite{dykes2007gwvis}. Local correlation has been shown
varying with scale and geography~\cite{goodwin2016multiple}, and local indicators of
spatial association decompose global statistics into per-observation
contributions~\cite{anselin1995lisa}. The modifiable areal unit problem---results
that change with the scale and the zoning of units---is reviewed by
Openshaw~\cite{openshaw1984maup} and shown to be unpredictable in multivariate
analysis by Fotheringham and Wong~\cite{fotheringham1991maup}. For point patterns,
Ripley's $K$ counts events within growing distances of an arbitrary event, with
$K(t)=\pi t^2$ under a Poisson process and simulation envelopes for
inference~\cite{ripley1977modelling}; second-order neighbourhood analysis takes the
same view from each individual point~\cite{getis1987second}; and ring (O-ring)
statistics complement the cumulative circle~\cite{wiegand2004rings}. The
log--log slope of mass within distance of a chosen centre is also the mass--radius
dimension of fractal urban analysis~\cite{yamu2016fractal}.
\doubt{Getis \& Franklin and Wiegand \& Moloney were read at abstract level only;
a more canonical citation for radial (mass--radius) analysis should replace or
join Yamu et al., whose main subject is planning theory. Brunsdon et al.\ 2002 was read at abstract level; whether it discusses a
uniform kernel is unverified.}

These are the statistics a summarising lens computes, and we claim none of
them. Directed GW weighting in particular is a statistical precedent for
decomposing a neighbourhood by direction. What the lens contributes is a
\emph{placement} of that decomposition: each direction's summary is drawn at its
bearing on the lens boundary, and the radius control shows how the reading
depends on the radius before the analyst drags it.
